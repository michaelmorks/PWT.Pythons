REPTILE CARE & EDUCATION

Quarantine and Testing for your Next Reptile

Understanding the importance of quarantine, testing and responsible reptile keeping.

There are a number of viruses circulating in the reptile keeping community that can have either an immediate or long-term impact on collections if newly acquired animals are not effectively quarantined and virus testing is not conducted. Despite extensive research, a handful of these viruses currently have no cure. Many of these viruses can silently infect entire collections quickly without any animals showing signs of illness and can remain dormant for years before detection if initial quarantine and testing are not undertaken. Once active, a virus can act quickly, and many keepers have lost entire collections in an alarmingly abbreviated period of time. Below is a list of the most common viruses found in the Australian reptile community and information on effective quarantine practices.

Common Snake-Specific Viruses

Bornavirus

Bornavirus is one of the hardest to detect in its early stages and results in severe encephalitis (inflammation of the brain tissue). Symptoms include neurological decline, loss of reflexes and lethargic behaviour.

Sunshinevirus

Sunshinevirus can cause both neurological and respiratory disease and is often misdiagnosed as a respiratory infection if virus testing is not undertaken. Symptoms may include, but are not limited to, wheezing, open-mouth breathing and neurological disorders such as paralysis and abnormal coiling or twisting of the head or body.

Nidovirus

Nidovirus is associated with inflammation of mucous membranes. It causes respiratory infection and is closely related to coronavirus in humans. Symptoms include excessive mucus and wheezing. Extreme cases report mouth rot and cloudy eyes.

These viruses can lie dormant for prolonged periods of time, only becoming active when the immune system is compromised. Once active, they are highly contagious, spreading through entire collections. For infected pythons, the outcome is almost always fatal, although asymptomatic individuals can carry and transmit the virus without showing symptoms.

Non-viral and Parasite Infections

Some bacteria are associated with the above viruses, particularly as they show up on the same virus-positive test results. However, they can also return a positive result without the presence of a pathogen. In all cases, these opportunistic bacteria are common in small amounts but fluctuate in response to the immune system.

Although naturally present across most species (ourselves included), very little is known about their relationship with reptiles. This lack of information often sparks misunderstanding and panic when receiving a positive PCR or blood test result.

Mites

Seen as tiny dark specks often clustered around eyes, floating in water bowls or crawling across skin and scales, mites can spread rapidly through entire collections without proper quarantine practices. By feeding on blood, they can cause weight loss and anaemia if left untreated.

Mycoplasma

Often associated with respiratory disease and other primary viruses, research shows that ever-present mycoplasma levels can flare up due to poor husbandry, physical damage, wounds or infections. It is during this increase in bacteria that the immune system is low and a python is most susceptible to contracting a virus, or a dormant pathogen may become active.

Pseudomonas

Another opportunistic bacterium, typically associated with external infections and wounds. Pseudomonas is common in tiny amounts and harmless until the immune system is weakened by infection or improper enclosure conditions, when increased levels can lead to fatal infections. It is during this time, when the immune system is weakened, that the risk of both bacterial and viral infection can increase, but neither is directly caused by the other.

Bacterial Dermatitis

Caused by excessive moisture and poor hygiene, this bacterial skin infection, or scale rot, can be easily avoided through proper husbandry and care. Symptoms vary but most commonly include cracked, crusty scales, loss of scales or lesions, scale discolouration and a foul smell from the infected areas (most typically on the underside).

A highly contagious fungal infection with similar symptoms has been identified in wild reptile populations across southeast Queensland. This disease, in its advanced stages, is debilitating and fatal, with similar strains overseas decimating wild populations. The University of Queensland advises that if sick-looking reptiles are found, they should be reported to wildlife rescue or snake catchers. It is vital that, unless trained to do so, you avoid handling wild reptiles.

Setting Up a Quarantine Area

The most effective way to set up a quarantine area is to use a completely different space, separate from the rest of the collection. This may all seem daunting to private keepers with limited resources, but responsible practices will save your current reptiles in the event of a virus or infection. An area as far as possible from the main collection is recommended, with no shared air space or equipment.

Anyone entering the area should not have had same-day contact with any other reptiles, and all materials (paper towel, F10 bottle, tongs and hooks) should be kept with the quarantined animal and not used for any other purpose aside from managing the individual(s) in quarantine. Gloves should be worn, and the correct disinfecting and hand-washing procedures should be common practice. Proper precautions, such as showering and changing clothes between rooms or locations, will ensure no virus, bacteria or parasites can easily move to another host.

Where possible, use items that are disposable (hides, bowls, etc.). Non-porous materials made from plastic are ideal as they are easier to clean and disinfect. Use an easy-to-clean, sanitary substrate such as paper towel or butcher's paper.

A diluted disinfectant containing F10 is the most recommended product due to its ability to disinfect against the majority of viruses, fungi and bacteria encountered in reptile veterinary practices. It is important to thoroughly clean an area prior to using disinfectant, as organic waste can render disinfectants ineffective. It is also important to carefully follow the instructions for dilution and use, to ensure effectiveness and safe handling of the product, for you and your reptiles.

Any second-hand enclosures should be completely stripped and washed with a diluted bleach solution, left to dry, and then followed up with F10. The use of second-hand enclosures comes with the risk of infection to your collection.

How Long Should I Quarantine My Reptile?

The answer to this varies based on the reason for quarantine. Some infectious diseases can go for long periods of time without any symptoms showing. For other infections, such as mites or scale rot, a quarantine period of 90 days will be sufficient to allow the keeper to observe for signs of infestation before introducing (or reintroducing) the reptile into the collection. The recommended period of quarantine for any new reptile varies between 3, 6 and 12 months depending on the source.

Unfortunately, in some cases, viruses can take years to cause visible illness, or individuals may be asymptomatic. There are cases where individuals have been testing positive for years without showing any signs of disease. This means that they can be transmitting a virus for prolonged periods of time without showing any effects. Therefore, it is vital that testing is performed on the reptile prior to them leaving a quarantine situation, ideally with one test at the beginning and one at the end.

Enclosure Setups and Design

Creating naturalistic environments that support the health, wellbeing and natural behaviours of Carpet Pythons.

Like the decision to get any pet, bringing a Carpet Python (or any reptile) into your life can be incredibly rewarding, but it requires your commitment to ensure quality of life and husbandry requirements are met or exceeded. This is only a very basic insight into keeping pythons, and additional research should be done before picking and obtaining your new or first addition to the collection. Here at PWT Pythons, I aim to support natural behaviours; to do so, I design my enclosures from the ground up (literally) to suit my reptiles according to their locality and the known habitat of their wild counterparts.

Enclosure Sizes

Hatchlings

Sistema tubs are a great choice for hatchling and yearling Carpet Pythons, ideally >14L options to allow for a proper heat gradient. Hatchling tubs should have a hide at each end, a water bowl at the cool end, and paper towel is the preferred substrate for easy cleaning, but a natural substrate can be used if preferred. Underbelly heating, such as a heat mat or cord, should be used and must be connected to a temperature controller thermostat. Overhead heating or basking lamps above a tub are not recommended, as they do not allow for a temperature gradient either. All heat sources should be outside of the tub, on a fire-safe and stable surface, and should only ever cover one third of the base of the tub. The temperature probe from your thermostat should be either inside the hide or on the substrate above the heat.

Juveniles

As your hatchling or yearling python grows, you will need to consider upgrading enclosure sizes to meet the required enclosure standards. There are several options and, based on practicality, aesthetics or lifestyle, you may choose to upgrade sizes as your young python develops or move them straight into an adult-sized enclosure. The latter can be extremely rewarding when observing natural behaviours, but increased clutter and hiding spaces should be favoured over frequent handling. A simple increase in Sistema tub size or a smaller enclosure allows for easy cleaning and handling in a simplistic setup. I personally prefer a 75cm wide x 120cm high x 70cm deep enclosure for housing juveniles, where plenty of cover and hiding spaces prevent young pythons from feeling vulnerable and defensive before they grow to better fit the size.

Adults

The standard (or accepted) minimum for an adult Carpet Python is 120cm wide x 60cm high x 60cm deep. This size is often called a 4ft enclosure. Whilst this size is acceptable within the hobby, adult pythons typically exceed 4 feet in length, meaning that a 4ft enclosure will likely restrict movement and overall quality of life (QOL). The Morelia clade are a semi-arboreal species, and pythons in captivity seem to do best when this is taken into consideration. Although slightly more costly, custom-built enclosures give you the freedom to choose any size that suits your space and needs.

For reference, I choose to keep my adults in 150cm wide x 120cm high x 70cm deep enclosures, to align seamlessly with my juvenile enclosures. These almost square dimensions allow me to cater enclosures towards both arboreal and ground-dwelling behaviours, depending on the occupant.

Enclosure Setup

With time, you may find that one particular python prefers to stay on the ground while others may be more arboreal, prefer to soak in larger water bowls or spend a lot of time hiding. It is in our best interest (and that of your python) to create an enclosure that supports these behaviours. Whether you are going for something simplistic, naturalistic or fully bioactive is entirely up to you. Each of these options has its own pros and cons depending on your interests and needs.

Simple setups are functional, easy to clean and maintain, whilst being accessible if frequent handling is your goal. Naturalistic setups give freedom of choice and an enjoyable aesthetic without the responsibility of maintaining an ecosystem, a personal favourite with the locality collection. Bioactive options can be a rewarding, albeit challenging, choice, as a 'look but don't touch' style of enclosure setup that never fails to turn heads.

Heating

Whilst heating and a proper thermal gradient are vital for all reptiles in captivity, the requirements can vary slightly, even within subspecies, based on their natural habitat. Although a hardy species generally, I have found that 32°C on the warm side and around 20°C on the cool side seems to best suit most Carpet Pythons (although variegata and Bredli appear to thrive with temperatures set a few degrees higher).

This allows for a warmer basking spot as well as day/night fluctuations based on your location. By researching seasonal temperature averages for their natural environment, enclosures can be catered to suit your pythons based on their locality or subspecies.

Overhead heating in the form of basking globes and heat emitters provides basking opportunities, while heat cords and mats can provide additional heat if needed. This is especially beneficial at low levels when positioned under hides used by defensive or flighty pythons that might otherwise seem stressed while basking out in the open.

With the use of vertical enclosures like my 120x75cm, a heat gradient is created from top to bottom, in comparison to lengthwise with horizontal or larger square enclosure dimensions. Remember that Carpet Pythons (and all reptiles) cannot regulate their body temperature and instead need to move to warmer or colder zones as required.

Lighting

Whilst not crucial, appropriate ultraviolet (UV) lighting is important for quality of life and stimulating natural behaviours, aiding in mineral balance and supporting a healthy immune system. But there are two important UV types that can benefit Carpet Pythons in captivity. These are UVB (medium wave) and UVA (long wave).

Most reptile lighting bulbs on the market provide both UVB and UVA, but always check the box for more information to make sure that product is right for your setups.

My Process with Globe Setups

When designing my enclosures, I choose to use three fittings to give my Carpet Pythons everything they need to live a healthy life. A UVB and a basking globe (50–100W) are powered by timers, whilst a deep heat emitter provides ample heating when needed (without emitting light). This is connected to a thermostat.

Over winter here in Victoria, the heat emitters will kick on overnight without creating any light, but during summer, most days the basking globes will give enough heat on their own to maintain the required temperature.

For my smaller 120x75, I only need to use a 75W basking globe, and the 120x150 enclosures are fitted with 100W globes, but this is plenty. Different reptile species and different enclosure setups have different requirements.

Decorating and Design

When setting up your enclosure, the options can seem endless… artificial rock backgrounds, vinyl prints, fake plants and live plants, substrates, water bowls, perches, branches and ground cover. There are termite mounds, stand-alone rocks, hollow logs, even twine hammocks and artificial skulls. How you decorate your enclosures is entirely up to you, and for every real rock or plant there is an artificial alternative to suit your needs. If you are like me, naturalistic is the way to go and, with a locality-focused collection like mine, the challenge of recreating each natural habitat has become one of my favourite aspects of keeping reptiles.

To recreate habitats, I have chosen to use vinyl-printed backgrounds of photographed habitats along with natural substrate, ground cover and real branches. Logs and branches should be sourced ethically; I usually reach out to my local council asking for suitable branches that have been cut down, or alternatively, I have a network of family properties. Branches, logs and especially hollows should never be taken from the bush, potentially disturbing native wildlife or destroying vital habitat.

With all wood, I either soak or spray it with F10 before sealing it with a large garbage bag and leaving it in the sun for a few weeks. This kills any nasty stowaways before they can wreak havoc in an enclosure. That being said, I do bring fresh leaf cuttings in on a weekly basis to provide enrichment (which I will cover in the article below), and I am sure that the occasional insect or natural bacteria briefly becomes part of the enclosure ecosystem.

For ground cover, I use these same leaves once they have dried out, to create a natural layer of leaf litter without going through the sterilising process that I use for branches and logs.

I have also begun exploring the use of live plants and artificial rocks in a non-bioactive but naturalistic setup. More detail on this and other aspects of my enclosure builds can be found on the PWT Pythons YouTube channel.

Water Bowls

There are as many different brands, styles and options for water bowls as there are Australian reptile species. But I have found that unless you are using disposable options, the size and weekly cleaning of your water bowls should be a priority when weighing up your options.

A water bowl should be large enough for your python to fully submerge, while also having a smooth internal surface for easy cleaning. Bowls are available in ceramic, metal, plastic and even resin options, or, if you are feeling creative, could be made much the same way as artificial rocks, with the addition of a pond sealant to hold water without leaking.

Although this last option is a contradiction, with the rough surfaces being very difficult to properly clean. A challenge that I myself have spent months attempting to overcome. Even so, the aesthetic and naturalistic look of an artificial rock water bowl in a suitable habitat is worth the trouble.
