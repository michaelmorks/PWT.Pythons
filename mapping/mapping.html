The Evolution of Carpet Pythons

Understanding the evolutionary journey of Morelia spilota

The Carpet Python (Morelia spilota) group is recognised as one of the most ecologically diverse, with several distinct distributions occupying a range of habitats across mainland Australia, many of its islands and southern New Guinea (Natusch et al. 2026) (Esqueré et al. 2020). However, to understand the incredible diversity that we see in Australian pythons today, we need to understand a journey that began almost 60 million years ago. An impossible task, until a study published in 2020 used Biogeography, Phylogenomics and Morphometrics to describe the evolution of Australo-Papuan pythons (Esqueré et al. 2020).

This article details the findings of that study below.

The Eocene (56–33.9 million years ago)

The dawn of a new world, and like many present-day animals, biogeographic reconstruction places the origin of pythons in Asia during this time.

The Oligocene (33.9–23 million years ago)

The Oligocene epoch saw a global decrease in temperatures and the spread of grasslands and deserts. During this global shift, ancestral python groups had separated, and in the process, some had dispersed into Africa, while the Indo-Australian lineage dispersed across the Wallace Line and were living in what we now call Indonesia.

By the early Miocene (23.3–15 million years ago), some groups had travelled back across the Wallace Line, to disperse across SE Asia. While what was now the Australo-Papuan lineage continued their insular dispersal, hopping from island to island before eventually dispersing across New Guinea.

The Mid Miocene (15–11 million years ago)

During this sub-epoch, the Australo-Papuan lineage separated into two groups/clades: Morelia and Bothrochilus. The latter primarily remained and diversified in New Guinea, with some dispersing into Australia or back to Indonesia. The Morelia group moved further south, utilising a land bridge between New Guinea and Australia (known as Sahul).

The clade made three separate ventures back into New Guinea before glacial melting increased sea levels by 120m, isolating the two land masses. These three dispersals became what we now know as the Spotted Python, Papuan Carpet Python and Green Tree Python.

The Mid Pleistocene (1.8 million years ago)

By the mid Pleistocene (1.8 million years ago), each of these diversified clades had settled or become bound by their respective geographic situations, allowing ample time between then and now to adapt to specific ecological niches across the planet. For most of the Morelia clade, mainland Australia’s unforgiving landscapes and its many habitats became home.

Adaptive Radiation and Environmental Change

A handful of papers discuss adaptive radiation and its association with ecological opportunities. Especially those found on islands, or in some cases, continents that have experienced suitable periods of isolation (Esqueré et al. 2020) (Wagner et al. 2012) (Losos JB. 2010).

As the Australo-Papuan lineage dispersed across the landmass that would someday become Australia and New Guinea, they found an ecologically diverse landscape. At the time, NG was predominantly rainforest, as was much of Northern Australia (including Uluṟu), likely giving rise to an early form of Morelia, possibly resembling present-day Rough-scaled Pythons (Morelia carinata).

Biogeographic reconstruction would suggest that this species was one of the first to diverge from the clade, around 17 million years ago. Although Narawan oenpelliensis had diverged a few million years prior (Esqueré et al. 2020).

As the planet warmed up and regions further from the equator began to dry out, what was rainforest became open woodland and later, scrubland or desert (McLaren et al. 2014). As the environment shifted, the group that separated away from early Rough-scaled pythons split again, giving rise to what would become Morelia spilota and Morelia viridis, the latter becoming split between New Guinea and the rainforests of present-day Cape York Peninsula with the rise of global sea levels (Esqueré et al. 2020).

Modern Carpet Pythons

Having diverged from their rainforest counterparts, a new group of pythons found themselves adapting to a harsher, drier and, in the south, colder environment. This group would go on to become the present-day Morelia spilota.

These vital adaptations to a changing climate allowed the clade to reach new ecological opportunities, which in turn, gave rise to further morphological diversification. These newly available ecological niches have contributed to the phenotypic variation of subspecies that we see in Carpet Pythons today.
